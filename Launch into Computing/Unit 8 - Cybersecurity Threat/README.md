Security Risk Assessment – Aurora Aesthetics Clinic

Introduction and Business Background

Aurora Aesthetics Clinic is a hypothetical medium-sized beauty and aesthetics business operating three branches. The clinic provides treatments such as facial procedures, laser treatments and other non-surgical aesthetic services. Its cloud-based management system is used for appointment bookings, staff schedules, payment information and confidential client records, including medical histories, allergies, previous treatments and consultation notes. A cybersecurity incident begins when a receptionist receives an email appearing to come from the clinic’s booking software provider. The email asks the employee to verify their account through a supplied link. Believing the message to be genuine, the receptionist enters their username and password on a fraudulent website. The attacker then uses the stolen credentials to gain unauthorised access to the clinic’s system.

Identified Threats and Vulnerabilities
The initial phishing attack creates the opportunity for a more serious ransomware and data-extortion attack. After gaining access, the attacker could download confidential client records and introduce ransomware into the clinic’s network. Instead of only encrypting the files, the attacker could threaten to publicly release sensitive medical and treatment information unless a ransom is paid. This could include information about clients’ health conditions, medications, allergies or cosmetic procedures. The clinic may be particularly vulnerable if staff accounts have excessive permissions, multi-factor authentication is not enabled, security patches are outdated, or backups are connected directly to the main network. Such an incident could interrupt appointments and payments while also causing serious reputational damage and loss of client trust.

Actionable Cybersecurity Strategy
The clinic should adopt several practical security controls. First, multi-factor authentication (MFA) should be mandatory for every staff account, meaning that a stolen password alone would not normally be sufficient to gain access. Second, client information should be encrypted both at rest and in transit, protecting data stored in databases as well as information moving between devices and cloud servers. Access should follow the principle of least privilege, ensuring receptionists, therapists and managers can access only the information required for their jobs. The clinic should also use firewalls, endpoint security, regular software patching and continuous monitoring to identify suspicious activity. Regular offline or isolated backups would support recovery following ransomware. Staff should receive periodic phishing-awareness training and be encouraged to report suspicious emails immediately.

Professional and Legal Cybersecurity Frameworks
The clinic also has professional, ethical and legal responsibilities when handling confidential client information. Where GDPR applies, health-related information requires particularly careful protection, and organisations must implement appropriate technical and organisational measures to secure personal data. Access should be limited, unnecessary data should not be retained indefinitely, and serious breaches may require formal assessment and notification. ISO/IEC 27001 provides a professional framework for managing information-security risks through an Information Security Management System. Applying its principles would encourage the clinic to identify risks systematically, document controls, assign responsibilities and regularly review security performance. Together, legal compliance and recognised security standards can help the clinic protect clients, demonstrate accountability and maintain trust.
